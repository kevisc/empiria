Dear Dr Jones,

Thank you for the constructive decision letter on my earlier submission
(manuscript 5250955, "Empiria: A Composable, Browser-Based Environment for
Simulation-Based Statistics Education"). The three concerns you raised have
driven a substantial revision, which I am pleased to submit for your
consideration. I have addressed each of your points directly.

**1. Expanded *Illustrative classroom use*.** The previous short paragraph has
been replaced by six detailed lesson walkthroughs. For each of the law of
large numbers, the meaning of "95% confidence", the bootstrap sampling
distribution, Welch's two-sample *t*, building a data-generating process from
interchangeable parts, and false positives under the null, the revised text
gives the target audience, the mode of use (in-lecture demonstration,
computer lab, homework, group exploration), an approximate duration, what the
student does and observes step by step, and the misconception or learning
objective the activity targets.

**2. A new *Teaching and learning context* section.** Inserted before the
lessons, this section identifies the three audiences the tool serves
(general-education and first quantitative courses; introductory undergraduate
statistics across the social and life sciences; methods courses for advanced
undergraduate and postgraduate students), the modes of use the lessons
support, and the pacing and preparation an instructor can expect (typical
activities 10 to 45 minutes; preparation on the order of ten minutes per
lesson; no installation on lab machines).

**3. A new *Informal feedback and design evolution* section.** The revised
manuscript describes how the tool and the paper changed in response to
informal practitioner input gathered during development: students who tried
the running application, and a small set of colleagues teaching introductory
statistics who responded to an open call for impressions, raised two recurring
themes. The first was *applicability* to a specific course's teaching goals;
the second was the *activation cost* of a node-and-cable interface for
first-time users. The manuscript now traces two concrete design responses:
the new *Teaching and learning context* section and the expanded lessons
address the applicability concern, and a guided four-step tour added to the
application itself addresses the on-ramp concern. Because the input was
solicited as service-improvement feedback rather than as human-subjects
research, the section reports themes in aggregate form only; no identifying
or quantified data are presented, and a brief note to that effect appears in
the *Disclosure statement*. The *Limitations* are explicit on what this is
and is not: Empiria has not been studied in a classroom or experimental
setting and we make no claim about its effect on student understanding; a
formal evaluation is identified as a possible future extension.

Two smaller changes followed the same line of feedback. The *Computational
footprint* paragraph has been condensed, with the full benchmark moved into
the Supplementary Materials, in keeping with the journal's preference to
minimize formal material in the body. The numerical-correctness story is
unchanged: closed-form routines remain verified against R to machine precision
by an automated suite of 41 checks, with an independent R reference script
that a reviewer can rerun in minutes; the full validation table sits in the
Supplementary Materials, with the in-text section pointing to it.

A brief word on context. I am a postdoctoral researcher at the Institute of
Communication and Public Policy at USI, where my work concerns quantitative
methods in the social sciences. Empiria grew out of an unexpected overlap
between that work and a long-standing hobby of mine, patching modular
synthesizers. A modular synth is a live, visual, tactile computer: you build
a process by wiring small modules together and hear it change as you turn a
knob. That is also the spirit of the older analog-computing tradition, in
which a calculation was something physical you could see and adjust rather
than a hidden function call. Empiria brings that sensibility to statistics, so
that the inferential workflow becomes something a student assembles, touches,
and watches respond in real time. I have written the article for
practitioners, in the hope of adding to the conversation about hands-on and
alternative ways of conveying statistical ideas.

The manuscript is original, is not under consideration elsewhere, has a single
author with no competing interests and no funding to declare, and has been
prepared for double-blind review (a separate title page carries all
identifying information).
