I am pleased to submit an original article, "Empiria: A Composable,
Browser-Based Environment for Simulation-Based Statistics Education," for
consideration in *Teaching Statistics*.

Simulation-based reasoning—having students build a sampling distribution
empirically before they meet it as a formula—is now a mainstream goal of
statistics teaching, but the supporting software is typically siloed: each
applet addresses one concept and cannot pass its output to the next step, so
the workflow students must internalize stays implicit. The article introduces
Empiria, a free, open-source, browser-based environment that makes that
workflow explicit by representing it as a composable dataflow graph: students
wire small modules together and watch every intermediate quantity update in
real time. It requires no installation, runs on any modern device (including
Chromebooks and tablets), and ships sixteen ready-to-use lesson worksheets and
a short guided tour, so an instructor can adopt it without preparation.

I believe the article fits the journal's emphasis on good practice in teaching
in three ways. First, it offers a concrete, classroom-ready way to teach the
inferential workflow as a single connected picture rather than a sequence of
disconnected demonstrations, with bundled activities that span
general-education through introductory and graduate courses. Second, it is
grounded in the statistics-education literature on dynamic visualization and
simulation-based inference. Third, and unusually for a teaching tool, its
numerical routines use exact recipes rather than normal-theory approximations
and are verified against R to machine precision by an automated test suite,
with a script that regenerates every reference value independently—so the
numbers an instructor puts in front of students are auditable, not merely
plausible.

The article describes an educational technology and its design rationale; it
does not yet report a classroom evaluation, which it states explicitly and
frames as planned future work. The software is freely available, so reviewers
can use it directly and inspect its source and tests. As a disclosure, AI-based
coding assistants were used as programming aids during the software's
development; all statistical methods and results were specified by the author
and verified against the independent references described in the manuscript.

The manuscript is original, is not under consideration elsewhere, has a single
author with no competing interests and no funding to declare, and has been
prepared for double-blind review (a separate title page carries all
identifying information).
